The steering and drive system is one of the most important parts of Cheese because it decides whether the robot can move fast without losing control. The drive system moves the robot forward, while the steering system changes the angle of the front wheels so the robot can turn.

For a beginner, the steering and drive system can be understood as two different jobs working together:

<div align="center">

| System | Simple Explanation | Main Part | Why It Matters |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Drive system** | Makes Cheese move forward. | EV3 Large Motor | Provides the force needed to move the robot’s mass. |
| **Steering system** | Changes where Cheese points. | EV3 Medium Motor | Controls the angle of the front wheels. |
| **Steering linkage** | Connects the steering motor to the wheels. | Pins, axles, connectors, and liftarms | Transfers motor movement into wheel movement. |
| **Chassis support** | Holds everything in place. | Technic frame and beams | Prevents motors and sensors from shifting during turns. |

</div>

The drive motor and steering motor cannot be treated as separate systems. If the drive system moves too fast, the steering may not react in time. If the steering moves too sharply, the robot can overcorrect. This is why Cheese’s steering depends on both **mechanical structure** and **software tuning**.

<p align="center">
  <strong>Key idea:</strong><br>
  The drive motor moves Cheese forward, while the steering mechanism controls direction.
</p>

## ❀ 1.3.4 How the Steering Linkage Moves the Front Wheels ────୨ৎ────────୨ৎ────

The steering linkage is the mechanical path between the EV3 Medium Motor and the front wheels. The motor does not directly grab the wheels. Instead, it rotates a connected mechanism made of LEGO Technic connectors, pins, axles, and liftarms.

When the Medium Motor turns, the linkage pushes or pulls the steering arms. This changes the angle of the front wheels. If the motor turns one way, the wheels steer in one direction. If the motor turns the opposite way, the wheels steer in the other direction.

<div align="center">

| Step | What Happens | Why It Matters |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Motor rotates** | The EV3 Medium Motor turns. | Creates controlled steering motion. |
| **2. Linkage moves** | The connector and axle pieces transfer that motion. | Converts motor rotation into wheel angle change. |
| **3. Front wheels turn** | The wheels point left or right. | Changes the direction Cheese travels. |
| **4. Robot follows the curve** | The drive motor keeps pushing forward. | Steering and drive work together. |

</div>

The linkage must move freely enough for the motor to control it, but it must also be supported strongly enough to keep the wheel angles stable. If the linkage is too stiff, the motor struggles. If it is too loose, the wheel angle becomes unreliable.

## ❀ 1.3.5 How Ackermann Steering Works ────୨ৎ────────୨ৎ────

Cheese uses **Ackermann steering geometry** on the front axle. This means the two front wheels do not turn at the exact same angle during a curve.

When a vehicle turns, both front wheels rotate around the same turning center. However, the inner wheel is closer to that center, so it must follow a tighter circle. The outer wheel is farther away, so it follows a wider circle.

Because of this:

<p align="center">
  <strong>The inner wheel must turn more sharply than the outer wheel.</strong>
</p>

<p align="center">
  <img src="../../img/ackermann_diagram.png" alt="Ackermann steering diagram" width="65%">
</p>

<p align="center">
  <em>Ackermann steering diagram showing why the inner wheel follows a smaller radius and the outer wheel follows a larger radius.</em>
</p>

### ❀ Ackermann Formula Support ────୨ৎ────────୨ৎ────

The Ackermann formulas help explain why both front wheels should not turn at the same angle. During a curve, the inner wheel follows a smaller radius and the outer wheel follows a larger radius.

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Formula_Goal-Explain_Wheel_Angles-4A90E2?style=for-the-badge">
  <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Design_Reason-Reduce_Tire_Scrub-FFD43B?style=for-the-badge">
</p>

<div align="center">

| Measurement | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **R** | Turning radius measured to the center of the axle. |
| **T** | Track width, or the distance between the two front wheels. |
| **L** | Wheelbase, or the distance between front and rear axles. |
| **θ inner** | Steering angle of the inner wheel. |
| **θ outer** | Steering angle of the outer wheel. |

</div>

<p align="center">
  <strong>Inner wheel radius = R − (T / 2)</strong><br>
  <strong>Outer wheel radius = R + (T / 2)</strong>
</p>

<p align="center">
  <strong>tan(θ inner) = L / (R − T/2)</strong><br>
  <strong>tan(θ outer) = L / (R + T/2)</strong>
</p>

Because the inner wheel radius is smaller, the inner wheel angle must be larger.

<p align="center">
  <strong>θ inner &gt; θ outer</strong>
</p>

This is the key idea behind Ackermann steering: the inner wheel turns more sharply so both wheels can roll through the curve instead of dragging sideways across the mat.

### What happens without Ackermann steering

If both front wheels are forced to the same angle, one wheel is not aligned with the arc it is actually traveling. Instead of rolling cleanly, it can drag sideways across the mat. This is called tire scrub.

Tire scrub matters because it can:

<div align="center">

| Tire Scrub Effect | Why It Hurts Cheese |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Wastes energy** | More motor effort is lost to friction. |
| **Makes turns less predictable** | The robot may not follow the expected curve. |
| **Adds stress to the linkage** | Side forces can loosen pins and connectors. |
| **Affects repeatability** | One corner may not match the next corner. |

</div>

This is why Ackermann steering was selected instead of a simpler front-wheel setup.

## ❀ 1.3.6 Why Steering Stability Matters During Curves ────୨ৎ────────୨ৎ────

Steering stability is important because Cheese must turn corners while still reading sensors, correcting its path, and avoiding walls. If the steering mechanism is unstable, the robot can lose control even if the software is making the correct decision.

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Curve_Risk-Late_Turns_&_Oversteer-FF8FAB?style=for-the-badge">
  <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Stability_Need-Fixed_Linkage_&_Motor_Mounts-FFD43B?style=for-the-badge">
</p>

<div align="center">

| Steering Instability | What Can Happen |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Motor mount shifts** | The commanded angle may not match the real wheel angle. |
| **Linkage flexes** | The wheels can lag or move unevenly. |
| **Frame bends** | Sensors can shift and give less reliable readings. |
| **Robot is too top-heavy** | Turns can feel less controlled. |
| **Robot is too heavy** | Steering corrections need more time and force to control the robot’s path. |
| **Cables block the mechanism** | Steering movement can become restricted or harder to inspect. |

</div>

During curves, the steering system must react before the robot reaches the wall. If the wheels are still moving toward the required angle when Cheese enters the curve, the robot may turn too late. If the steering correction is too strong for the robot’s speed, the robot may oversteer.

This is why steering is not only a software issue. It is a mechanical system that depends on motor placement, linkage freedom, structure, weight, cable routing, and speed.

## ❀ 1.3.7 Building the Linkage: Failures and Fixes ────୨ৎ────────୨ৎ────

The steering linkage was one of the hardest mechanisms to make reliable. It had to be loose enough to move, but strong enough to keep its shape.

<div align="center">

| Version | Joint / Structure Used | Problem | Outcome |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **v1** | Technic liftarm 1×3 + pins | Too stiff; the motor struggled to move the linkage. | Scrapped. |
| **v2** | Connector with pin + 3L axle with stop | Steering moved more freely. | Kept as the working geometry. |
| **v3** | Same geometry as v2, reinforced | Snapping turns stressed the front structure. | Front assembly and motor mount were strengthened. |
| **v4** | Simplified surrounding structure with lighter chassis | Robot became lighter, lower, and cleaner. | Steering area became easier to inspect and less affected by unnecessary upper mass. |

</div>

### v1: The linkage was too stiff

Our original linkage used a **Technic liftarm 1×3**, Technic pins, and a **3L axle with stop**. It looked correct in the model, but when the medium motor tried to steer, the joint resisted too much.

The problem was that the linkage was rigid in the place where it needed to pivot freely. We were asking the steering motor to fight the resistance of the mechanism before it could even turn the wheels.

### v2: Replacing the joint

We replaced the Technic liftarm 1×3 with a **connector with pin**, while keeping the same 3L axle with stop. This helped the joint pivot more freely, allowing the medium motor to move the linkage properly.

This became the steering geometry that carried into later versions.

### v3: Reinforcing the front assembly

In v3, the geometry still worked, but aggressive steering movements stressed the front assembly. The steering motor and linkage needed a more stable foundation because a steering motor that shifts while steering creates angle errors.

The v3 improvement was not a full redesign of the geometry. It was a reinforcement of the structure holding the mechanism in place.

### v4: Reducing unnecessary structure around the mechanism

In v4, the steering geometry still needed support, but the rest of the robot became lighter and less cluttered. The robot mass was reduced from **888 g to 666 g**, cable routing became shorter, and unnecessary upper structure was removed.

This helped the steering system because the robot became easier to inspect and less top-heavy. A lighter robot gives the steering system a better mechanical foundation because there is less mass to redirect during corrections.

## ❀ 1.3.10 How the v4 Redesign Supports Steering ────୨ৎ────────୨ৎ────

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/V3-888g-FF8FAB?style=for-the-badge">
  <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Change-222g_Removed-FFD43B?style=for-the-badge">
  <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/V4-666g-57C785?style=for-the-badge">
</p>

The v4 redesign did not replace the steering geometry, but it improved the environment around the steering system. The robot became lighter, lower, cleaner, and easier to inspect. This matters because steering does not happen in isolation; it depends on the mass, structure, cable routing, and stability of the whole robot.

The steering mechanism does not move the full robot by itself, but it controls the direction of a moving mass. When the robot is heavier, taller, or more cluttered, steering becomes harder to control because the robot has more mass to redirect and more structure that can flex, shift, or interfere.

Using Newton’s Second Law:

<p align="center">
  <strong>F = m × a</strong>
</p>

Solving for acceleration:

<p align="center">
  <strong>a = F ÷ m</strong>
</p>

If the available force stays similar and the robot mass becomes lower, the robot can respond more easily. For steering, this means the robot has less mass to redirect during corrections.

<div align="center">

| v4 Mechanical Change | How It Helps Steering |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Mass reduced from 888 g to 666 g** | Less mass to redirect during steering corrections. |
| **Extra camera structure removed** | Lower center of gravity and less top-heavy behavior. |
| **Large Motor laid down into the chassis** | More compact layout and better use of space. |
| **EV3 Brick placed flatter** | Cleaner structure and easier inspection. |
| **Shorter cables** | Less clutter around moving parts and easier debugging. |
| **Lower structure** | More compact body during turns. |
| **Important support points kept** | Steering geometry still stays supported. |

</div>

The trade-off was important. We could not remove structure from the steering support just to make the robot lighter. Instead, we removed unnecessary upper structure while keeping the parts that protect steering alignment, motor stability, and wheel geometry.

<p align="center">
  <strong>v4 steering lesson:</strong><br>
  A lighter robot helps steering response, but only if the steering structure remains stable.
</p>

## ❀ 1.3.11 How Unnecessary Upper Structure Can Affect Turning Stability ────୨ৎ────────୨ৎ────

Unnecessary upper structure can affect turning stability because it adds mass and height above the main chassis. When the robot turns, this extra mass can make the robot feel harder to control. It can also make the chassis more cluttered, which makes inspection and repair harder.

In v3, Cheese had more upper structure around the camera and more cable clutter. This did not mean the robot could not work, but it made the system harder to inspect and more complicated to stabilize.

In v4, we simplified the upper structure and shortened cables. This helped make the robot cleaner and more compact.

<div align="center">

| Upper Structure Issue | Possible Effect During Turning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Extra height** | Can make the robot feel more top-heavy. |
| **Extra mass** | Gives steering more mass to redirect. |
| **Cable clutter** | Makes inspection and debugging harder. |
| **Bulky camera support** | Adds structure above the chassis that does not directly improve steering. |
| **Blocked mechanism view** | Makes it harder to notice if a linkage, pin, or cable is interfering. |

</div>

The goal of v4 was not only to make the robot look cleaner. The goal was to reduce unnecessary structure so the steering and drive systems could be easier to inspect, easier to tune, and less affected by extra mass.

## ❀ 1.3.12 The Steering Problem We Keep Chasing ────୨ৎ────────୨ৎ────

Steering has been one of the hardest subsystems to tune because it can fail in two opposite ways.

### Turning too little

On fast corners, the steering motor sometimes does not reach the commanded angle before the robot is already entering the turn. The wheels are still moving when Cheese needs them already pointed, so the robot can continue too straight and crash.

### Turning too much

At other points, the turns came out too sharp and violent. These snapping movements stressed the linkage and forced us to reinforce the front assembly and motor mount.

### Why both can happen

These failures look opposite, but they come from the same relationship:

<p align="center">
  <strong>Driving speed must match steering response time.</strong>
</p>

If the robot drives faster than the steering can react, the turn happens too late. If the steering correction is too aggressive for the speed, the robot can oversteer.

<div align="center">

| Problem | Mechanical Cause | Software / Tuning Response |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Turn happens too late** | Steering angle is not reached in time. | Adjust speed layers and earlier curve response. |
| **Turn is too sharp** | Steering correction is too aggressive. | Smooth angle changes and tune steering limits. |
| **Linkage stress** | Snapping movements load the structure. | Reinforce support and reduce unnecessary mass. |
| **Unstable curve exit** | Robot exits the curve at a poor angle. | Tune return-to-center behavior. |
| **Wall impact** | Steering and speed are not synchronized. | Match driving speed to steering response. |

</div>

The mechanical side improved through freer linkage movement, reinforcement, and v4 simplification. The remaining challenge is tuning: matching speed, steering angle, and curve timing so the robot can turn consistently without hitting the walls.

| **Steering joint** | v1 linkage was too stiff. | Changed to connector-with-pin joint. | Steering moved more freely. |

| **Robot mass** | Heavier v3 structure made the robot harder to manage. | Reduced mass to 666 g in v4. | Cleaner and lighter structure supported better control. |
| **Cable routing** | Long cables made inspection harder. | Shortened cables in v4. | Easier debugging around the mechanism. |
| **Upper structure** | Extra height affected balance and clutter. | Removed unnecessary camera structure. | Robot became lower and more compact. |
| **Curve behavior** | Steering sometimes happened too late or too sharply. | Continued software tuning. | Speed and steering response must be matched. |

</div>

This testing showed that steering and drive cannot be treated as separate systems. The drive motor determines how fast Cheese reaches a curve, while the steering system determines whether the robot can turn before reaching the wall. The chassis structure, mass, cable routing, and motor mounting all affect how well those two systems work together.

## ✦ Final Steering and Drive Summary ─── ⋆⋅☆⋅⋆ ───

The steering and drive system must work together: the drive motor moves Cheese forward, while the steering mechanism controls direction and helps the robot handle curves without losing stability.

Cheese uses the EV3 Large Motor for propulsion because driving requires torque and force to move the robot’s mass. It uses the EV3 Medium Motor for steering because steering requires faster and more precise angle changes. The Ackermann linkage allows the front wheels to follow different turning radii, reducing tire scrub and making curves more predictable.

The v4 redesign improved this system by reducing mass, removing unnecessary upper structure, shortening cables, and making the steering and drive mechanisms easier to inspect. These changes did not replace the steering geometry, but they improved the mechanical environment around it.

  Steering is not only about the motor. It depends on linkage freedom, structure, mass, stability, speed, cable clearance, and tuning all working together.
